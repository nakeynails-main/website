Gel and acrylics broke my nails. Here's exactly why.

The damage happens during application, not removal.

You finally quit gel for good. You're excited to see what your real nails look like underneath. And then you see them: thin, cracked down the sides, splitting at the edges. The salon told you gel was safe. But your nails are telling a different story.

Nail splitting from gel and acrylics isn't random bad luck. It's a direct result of how these products are applied to your nails. The pressure, the chemicals, the filing, the weight of the extension itself. Your nails didn't just break. They were systematically weakened over months of appointments.

The damage starts before the gel or acrylic even touches your nail bed. When technicians file your natural nail to create a textured surface for adhesion, they're removing the protective outer layer. This isn't a gentle buffing. It's aggressive filing that thins the nail plate and creates microscopic damage that spreads over time.

The pressure of extensions causes structural damage

Acrylics and gels add significant weight and stress to your nail structure. Your nails weren't designed to carry that much load for weeks at a time. When you wear extensions, every movement you make puts pressure on the nail bed below. The connection between the extension and your natural nail creates a pivot point where stress concentrates.

This is why nail splitting from gel and acrylics often happens at the seam where the extension meets your natural nail. That's the weakest point. The constant flexing, combined with the weight of the product, causes the nail plate to split vertically. Over time, these tiny cracks multiply and deepen.

The longer you wear extensions without a break, the more severe the splitting becomes. Two weeks in, you might not notice anything. Six months in, your nails are peeling in layers and splitting on the sides. The damage is cumulative. Each appointment makes the existing damage worse before new damage starts.

Chemical exposure weakens the nail from inside

The adhesives, primers, and gel formulas used in these manicures contain strong solvents and bonding agents. These chemicals don't just sit on your nail surface. They penetrate the nail plate and alter its structure at a molecular level. Nail splitting from gel and acrylics is partly chemical damage happening invisibly beneath the surface.

Methyl methacrylate (MMA) in acrylic formulas is particularly harsh. It bonds aggressively to your nail, which means removal is incredibly damaging. Even when technicians soak off acrylics properly, the chemical has already weakened the keratin structure of your nail. Gel is gentler during application, but the UV curing process and gel solvents still cause lasting changes to nail integrity.

When you finally remove the extensions, your nails aren't just thinner. They're structurally compromised. The nail plate is brittle, dehydrated, and prone to splitting because the protective lipid layer has been stripped away. This is why peeling off your gel was a mistake that your nails are still paying for.

The recovery actually takes longer than you think

Your nails grow about one millimeter per week. That means it takes roughly four months for a completely new, undamaged nail to grow in from the base. But splitting nails during those four months suggests the damage is still active. Even after you stop getting manicures, your nails might keep splitting as the old damaged nail grows out.

This happens because the structural weakness doesn't disappear overnight. Your nails need support during the recovery period. They need hydration, protection from further stress, and the right nutrients to rebuild keratin strength. Without that support, splitting continues even after you've quit gel.

The good news is that this is fixable. Stopping extensions is the first step, but it's not enough on its own. Your nails need active repair to rebuild the barrier function and reinforce the nail plate. It addresses the structural damage from gel and acrylics while you're growing out healthy nail. Your nails didn't break because they're weak. They broke because of what was done to them. And they can recover with the right approach.
